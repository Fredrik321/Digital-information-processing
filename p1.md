### Format, array shape, data type, units, range, and byte size
All images are in `.jpg` format.

The images are classified into six different bird-species categories:

- American Goldfinch: 143
- Barn Owl: 129
- Carmine Bee-eater: 131
- Downy Woodpecker: 137
- Emperor Penguin: 139
- Flamingo: 132

This gives a total of 811 images.

All images have the shape `(224, 224, 3)`. The data type is `uint8`, and the values are pixel values in the range 0-255. The uncompressed in-memory size can be calculated as follows:

`811 * 224 * 224 * 3 = 122,078,208 bytes`

The pixel values do not have a physical unit. They are 8-bit sRGB values, which are gamma encoded, so they are not linear measurements of light. The whole range from 0 to 255 is used in the dataset. The JPEG files on disk are 15,967,831 bytes in total (around 15.2 MiB), which is about 7.6 times smaller than the uncompressed array. This means the images have already been compressed with a lossy format before we got them.

### Sampling rate and spacing
The images are sampled on a regular grid with a spacing of 1 pixel in the horizontal and vertical image coordinates. The physical spacing in the photographed scene, camera distance, lens, and capture sampling rate are unknown because they are not included in the dataset metadata. All images have a resolution of 224 x 224 pixels and are stored using three colour channels. The distance from and angle to the camera are not controlled or documented, and they vary between images. Most pictures appear to be taken from a side angle. Although the images appear to be genuine photographs of birds, some are drawings. Therefore, spatial frequencies are reported in cycles per pixel rather than cycles per metre.
### Transformation experiment
For this experiment, the transformation used is Sobel edge detection.

**Prediction:** The Sobel edge detection will produce stronger and more
accurate responses at high-contrast features such as the beak and eye,
and weaker responses across the body where brightness changes are more
gradual. The strength of the bird's outline will also depend on the
contrast between the bird and its background. For example, a penguin's
white stomach will produce a weaker outline against a snowy background.

The result matched this prediction. The beak, eye, feather boundaries, and bird outline produced stronger Sobel responses than smooth body regions. The outline was weaker where the bird and background had similar brightness.

### Quantisation/resampling experiment
As detailed in the notebook, quantisation is performed by reducing the bit depth from 8 bits to 1 bit for an image from the dataset. The experiment includes both a prediction and observations about how this affects the images.

**Prediction:** Because the birds have features with sudden transitions rather than gradual ones, the birds will not be strongly affected until the bit depth is fairly low, while backgrounds with gradual colour changes will show artefacts much earlier.

This uses one selected image, so the result is illustrative rather than a claim about every image in the dataset. At low bit depths, banding and false colour regions appear, while the broad bird shape remains more recognisable than fine texture.

### Frequency-domain finding
The 2-D spectral power distribution shows that most power is concentrated at low spatial frequencies. These frequencies represent large-scale shapes, smooth backgrounds, and the overall bird structure. Higher frequencies represent edges, feather texture, and other fine detail. No single high-frequency component is shared by all species, so the spectrum describes image structure rather than providing a complete species identifier.

It should be noted that the frequency view in the notebook is made from the mean image of the whole dataset. When 811 images with different framing are averaged, the result becomes blurry, which is similar to applying a low-pass filter. Because of this, the view shows more power at low frequencies than a single image would. It says more about the dataset as a whole than about the details in one picture.

We also wanted to see if the spectrum could be used to tell the species apart. For each image we calculated the radially averaged power spectrum and used it as input to a random forest classifier, with 75% of the images for training and 25% for testing. The classifier got 46.8% accuracy on the test set, while guessing would give 16.7% with six classes. So the spectrum contains some information about the species, but not nearly enough to identify the bird by itself. The Downy Woodpecker was recognised best (recall 0.79), which might be because of its black and white feathers, and the American Goldfinch was recognised worst (recall 0.19).

### Reproducible loader
The notebook contains a loader consisting of two functions for loading and viewing the dataset, plus a third function for creating the baseline representation for P2. The loader improves reproducibility by sorting the image paths deterministically and assigning the images a fixed data type. The P2 baseline is the complete dataset represented as one array with shape `(811, 224, 224, 3)`, dtype `uint8`, values in the range 0-255, and an uncompressed size of 122,078,208 bytes. The loader also checks that every image has the shape `(224, 224, 3)` before calling `np.stack`, and gives an error if one does not. This way a changed or broken file will be noticed instead of changing the baseline without us knowing.

### Visual Informatics concern
One concern for this dataset is the variation in the birds' backgrounds. Inspection shows that the images were taken in different environments. For instance, this could cause problems if the data were to be used in machine learning, because a model might classify images based on visual elements such as foliage, branches, or snow rather than on the birds themselves.

### Integrity concern
The dataset lacks information about the images themselves. Although all images are labelled according to bird species, metadata such as the original capture conditions and preprocessing history is not provided. This is an integrity concern because it limits our ability to verify how the images were originally captured or processed. For example, although the images currently have the same dimensions, we cannot determine whether they originally had the same resolution or whether they were resized or otherwise processed before being included in the dataset. This also makes it difficult to determine what effect preprocessing may have had on the images.

### How the two concerns are connected
The background concern and the integrity concern are connected through how the images were prepared. The images seem to have been cropped, resized to 224 x 224 and saved as JPEG, but we do not know how this was done. How the images were cropped decides how much background is left around the bird, so some of the background differences between species might come from the preparation and not from where the birds actually live. Resizing and JPEG compression also change the high frequencies in the images, and those are what the Sobel and Fourier experiments look at. Since we do not know the preprocessing history, we cannot say for sure if a difference between species comes from the bird, the background or the preprocessing. This also matters for P2, because the baseline is already compressed, so any artefacts we measure in P2 will come on top of the JPEG artefacts that are already there.
